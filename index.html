02 Intuition before math

The Cube That Does Not Fall

Picture a ten centimeter cube of water hanging in a swimming pool. It does not sink. It does not rise. Gravity is pulling on it, so something must be holding it up.

That something is the water around it. The water below pushes up a little harder than the water above pushes down. The difference is exactly the cube's weight.

Switch each force off and watch what happens. Then slide the cube deeper. Both pushes grow, but the gap between them never changes.

04 The Pascal paradox

A Thin Tube Can Burst A Barrel

In the 1600s, the story goes, Blaise Pascal fitted a long thin tube into a sealed barrel and poured water into the tube. A few cups of water were enough to split the barrel.

Only height matters. The equation has no term for width, volume or shape. A lake and a drinking straw of the same depth give the same pressure at the bottom.

The cone's slanted walls carry part of the liquid's weight. The barrel's lid pushes down on the liquid below it. Nothing extra reaches the floor in the first case, and nothing is missing in the second.

05 Regime 1 · incompressible

Liquids Refuse To Squash

Water barely changes density when you squeeze it. Treat as a constant and the equation integrates to a straight line.

Ten meters of water add about one atmosphere. That is the rule of thumb every diver learns. At the bottom of the Mariana Trench, the straight line is still within a few percent, because seawater only gets about five percent denser down there.

07 Regime 2 · compressible, isothermal

Air Squashes, So The Rule Bends

Air is a gas. Climb higher and the air above you thins out, so each layer weighs less than the one below it. Density is no longer a constant. It follows the pressure.

  • Ideal gas law gives density from pressure and a fixed temperature.
  • Put it in the hydrostatic equation. Pressure now appears on both sides.
  • The solution is an exponential. Every H of altitude cuts the pressure by a factor of e.

08 Regime 3 · polytropic

Real Air Cools As You Climb

Hike up a mountain and the temperature drops, about 6.5 degrees per kilometer. So the isothermal assumption was a fair first guess and a poor final answer.

Let pressure and density follow and the same hydrostatic equation gives a profile that ends at a finite height.

The same equation holds up stars and gas giants. In a star, gravity points toward the center, so pressure must climb inward fast enough to carry the weight of everything above.
